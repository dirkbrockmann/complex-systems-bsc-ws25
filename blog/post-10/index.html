The Gray-Scott Model

In the second lecture on pattern formation we discussed the Turing Mechanism and gave a few examples of reaction diffusion systems that generated interesting spatio-temporal patterns. In the last slide I showed you the famous Gray-Scott model, defined by these equations for the two chemical species UUU and VVV:

∂tu=−uv2+F(1−u)+Du∂x2u\partial_t u = -uv^2 + F(1-u) + D_u \partial_x^2 u∂t​u=−uv2+F(1−u)+Du​∂x2​u ∂tv=uv2−(F+k)v+Dv∂x2v\partial_t v = uv^2 -(F+k)v + D_v \partial_x^2 v∂t​v=uv2−(F+k)v+Dv​∂x2​v

The reaction part was derived from the basic reactions of the Gray-Scott model:

U+2V→3VV→∅.U+2V\rightarrow 3V \qquad V\rightarrow \emptyset.U+2V→3VV→∅.

We discussed that this is similar to a simple infection process, the only difference is that in the Gray-Scott model two “infected” individuals VVV infect a susceptible individual UUU.

Here’s the explorable again, so you can explore different patterns, either by using the presets for the parameters FFF and kkk or by trying out your own values.

  1. Try identifying a new parameters set that generates an interesting pattern, and write down the parameter combination.
  2. Explain why you find that pattern expecially interesting or beautiful.
  3. There’s a great and well aged website on the Gray Scott model, set up by Robert Munafo. Visit the website and check it out. This will potentially help you identifying interesting patterns.

The Turing Mechanism

In class we discussed the Turing Mechanism. Because this is such a weird mechanism discovered by such a brilliant mind you are supposed to do the following also in an effort to prepare you well for the final exam:

  1. Explain the Turing Mechanism in plain words, supplemented by drawings of concentrations of particles involved.
